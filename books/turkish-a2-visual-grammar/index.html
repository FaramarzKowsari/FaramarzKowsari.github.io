About this combined A2 edition

Turkish A2 Visual Grammar is the combined A2 product containing the material originally organized as Books 6-11. The source examined so far for this landing page is a 150-page portion containing the material from original Books 6, 7 and 8. These sections move from noun and case architecture into modal meanings and then into habits and the Turkish aorist.

The teaching design is strongly visual and cumulative. Grammar is repeatedly broken into roots, suffixes, buffer consonants and personal endings, then rebuilt through short example sentences, dialogues, mini stories, review charts, practice arenas and end-of-level challenges. English support is used to make the Turkish structure visible while the recurring exercises push the learner toward producing Turkish rather than merely recognizing rules.

Original Book 6 material — Objects, Compounds & Deeper Nouns

The Book 6 portion develops the noun system beyond beginner-level forms. It begins with the definite object case -ı / -i / -u / -ü, contrasting a general noun such as kitap with a specific object such as kitabı. Vowel-final nouns introduce the buffer y, producing forms such as suyu, arabayı and elmayı. The learner also practices object questions such as Kimi? and Neyi?, then builds full subject-object-verb sentences.

The next sequence teaches compound nouns through forms such as Türk kahvesi, okul çantası, telefon numarası and ev kapısı. It distinguishes a compound that names a type from a personal possessive form, then shows how a compound can take another case ending. This leads naturally into possessive + case chains: kitabımda, kitabımı, evime, evimden, odasında and arabasından, including the buffer n that connects possessive endings to following case suffixes.

Location is expanded through içinde, üstünde, altında, yanında, önünde and arkasında. The suffix -ki then turns locations into modifiers, as in evdeki kitap, and is also introduced in ownership forms such as benimki and seninki. The section closes with comparison and superlative patterns using daha and en, special forms such as daha iyi and daha kötü, and an explicit sentence-order pattern built around Time + Place + Object + Verb.

Original Book 7 material — Ability, Necessity & Obligation

The Book 7 portion organizes modal meanings into a practical progression. Ability is introduced with -ebil / -abil, including vowel harmony, person forms, negative ability, and questions such as Gelebilir misin? and Yüzebilir misin?. The same structure is then used for polite permission — Girebilir miyim? — and for possibility, as in Yağmur yağabilir.

The necessity section compares several ways of expressing need. lazım appears in forms such as Gitmem lazım; gerek / gerekiyor expresses that something is necessary; zorunda olmak introduces stronger obligation such as Gitmek zorundayım; and ihtiyacım var / yok expresses having or not having a need. By placing these forms side by side, the course makes their differences visible rather than treating every English “need” or “have to” as the same Turkish structure.

Advice is taught with -malı / -meli, including positive, negative and question forms such as çalışmalısın, yapmamalısın and Ne yapmalıyım?. Suggestions then shift into Hadi gidelim and Sinemaya gidelim mi?, while polite desire is practiced through Bir çay isterim. Airport, clinic, help-desk and team-planning mini stories combine ability, need, obligation, advice, permission and polite requests in situations that resemble everyday communication.

Original Book 8 material — Habits & Aorist

The Book 8 portion introduces the Turkish aorist / geniş zaman as a system for habits, routines, repeated actions and general truths. It explicitly contrasts an action happening now with a recurring habit, helping learners distinguish the present continuous -yor from aorist forms such as gelirim, yaparsın, içer, gideriz and çalışırlar.

The course separates major aorist suffix patterns — -r, -ar / -er and -ır / -ir / -ur / -ür — and practices them across persons and common verbs. It then introduces negative aorist forms such as gelmem, yapmazsın and bilmez, followed by questions such as Gelir misin? and polite request patterns such as Yardım eder misin?.

The later lessons connect the aorist to frequency expressions and real routines: her gün, genellikle, bazen, hiç, sabahları and hafta sonları. It is also used for general truths, preferences, invitations and open promises. A dedicated comparison distinguishes the aorist from the future tense, for example the difference between a general or open Ararım and the more specific planned Arayacağım. Mini stories, family habits, café routines, review charts and a final boss-style review consolidate the material before it continues into the original Book 9 section of the combined A2 edition.

What learners practice in the reviewed portion

  • Definite-object marking and the difference between general and specific nouns
  • Buffer consonants y and n in natural suffix chains
  • Compound nouns and the difference between type and personal possession
  • Possessive forms followed by accusative, locative, dative and ablative cases
  • Location expressions with inside, on, under, next to, in front of and behind
  • -ki location modifiers and ownership forms
  • Comparatives and superlatives with daha and en
  • Natural word order with time, place, object and verb
  • Ability, inability, permission and possibility with -ebil / -abil
  • Necessity and obligation with lazım, gerek, zorunda olmak and ihtiyacım var / yok
  • Advice, negative advice and advice questions with -malı / -meli
  • Suggestions, polite requests and isterim
  • Aorist forms for routines, habits and general truths
  • Aorist negatives, questions, preferences, invitations and promises
  • Frequency expressions and the contrast between aorist, present continuous and future forms

Who this book is for

For learners who have moved beyond basic Turkish and want to make suffix combinations, sentence structure and everyday modal meanings more automatic. The visual design is especially suited to learners who remember grammar better when they can see a word being assembled step by step and then immediately apply it in a short sentence, dialogue or story.
