QQQ bounced. Which high would change your mind?

Pull up a daily QQQ chart from March 2 through April 3, 2020, and hide everything to the right. This is the window used in Chapter 22, Figures 34 and 35. Mark the swing high the recovery cleared. In a second color, mark the high that still matters to someone reading the larger decline. Would crossing the first level settle the question you asked of the second?

Compare your reading with the book’s

The smaller swing gives the rebound a foothold. The larger high tells you whether the bearish reading still holds. If that high survives, two traders can disagree about direction and both have a coherent reading. Ask each to mark the level that would change their answer. Now you can see whether they disagree about the evidence or simply about the scale.

Try it with a trading partner. Mark up the same window separately, then swap charts before either of you reveals the next bar. In the book, the unmarked QQQ chart is on page 122 and its annotated companion is on page 123.

About this book

Market Structure Trading organizes price into meaningful swing hierarchy instead of labeling every pivot equally. It develops continuation through BOS, early warning through CHoCH and a stricter MSS standard for cases where the old structural map becomes a worse explanation of price. Internal and external structure, protected highs and lows, trend, range and transition states give each label a decision role.

Liquidity sweeps, displacement, momentum, Fair Value Gaps, dealing ranges, equilibrium, premium/discount and top-down analysis are added as contextual layers. The book repeatedly warns that a sweep is not proof of reversal and that terminology differs across practitioner communities; what matters is stating the convention, defining invalidation and building one coherent map whose assumptions can be abandoned when the evidence changes.

Inside the complete Market Structure Trading guide

Market Structure Trading develops a defined progression from Price Is Not Random Noise through Liquidity Through the Lens of Structure to Complete Market Structure Case Studies. Rather than treating each term as a ready-made signal, the original contents invite readers to connect the market observation, its surrounding context and the conditions under which it would stop being useful. Questions readers ask

Which charts can I practice on?

Chapter 22 includes ten historical cases across SPY, QQQ, GLD, USO and HYG. Each pairs a raw daily chart with an annotated companion so you can record a reading before comparing it with the author’s.

Where should I start if I already know BOS and CHoCH?

Try the ten exercises beginning on page 136. Grade a break, choose a dealing range and define what would invalidate your reading. Each exercise has a reasoning guide for checking the decision you made.

How does the book handle conflicting timeframes?

Chapter 17 examines a countertrend move inside a larger trend. The QQQ case on pages 122–123 gives you a historical chart on which to distinguish a local recovery from a change in the larger structure.

What you will learn

  • Rank swings by structural consequence rather than visual drama.
  • Differentiate BOS, CHoCH and MSS according to continuation, warning and replacement roles.
  • Use liquidity and displacement as contextual evidence rather than automatic signals.
  • Integrate FVGs, dealing ranges, premium/discount and top-down analysis without losing hierarchy.
  • Define invalidation so a structural thesis can genuinely fail instead of being renamed after the fact.
