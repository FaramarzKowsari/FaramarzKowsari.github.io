Learn what the exposure map can tell you, and what it cannot

A volatility crush follows an earnings release. An options dashboard displays a large exposure concentration. The index then rallies, and a familiar explanation appears: dealers were forced to buy. It is an attractive story. Yet it quietly skips several questions. Which volatility contracts changed? Did the volatility surface shift or rotate? Which positions were actually held by dealers? Was the inferred hedge ever executed?

VANNA & CHARM EXPOSURE TRADING by Faramarz Kowsari starts with those missing links. It explains how an option's delta responds not only to an underlying-price change but also to a move in implied volatility and the passage of time. It then shows how a model's Greek sensitivities can be aggregated under explicitly stated inventory assumptions, compared with price behavior and subjected to more rigorous testing.

Rather than promising a secret signal, the book gives traders a research framework. Its 26 chapters move through volatility surfaces, VEX and Charm exposure maps, short-dated options, OPEX, index-futures hedging, risk-bounded trade setups and reproducible Python experiments. Every major mechanism is paired with an applied exercise intended to separate theoretical arithmetic from an empirical market consequence.

A particularly valuable discipline runs through the book: observed option data are not the same as modeled exposure; modeled exposure is not known dealer inventory; a hypothetical hedge adjustment is not evidence of an actual order. For traders surrounded by confident explanations of hidden market flows, that distinction changes how information is evaluated.

Before asking where dealer hedging might push the market, ask what was observed, what was calculated, what inventory was assumed and what would prove the hypothesis wrong.

Three forces that can change delta

Local delta-change model

dΔ ≈ Γ dS + Vanna dσ + Charmt dt

Gamma describes delta's sensitivity to spot price. Vanna measures delta's sensitivity to implied volatility. Calendar-time Charm measures delta's response to time passing. Each requires a defined pricing model, units and sign convention. Real changes can also contain higher-order effects.

Vanna: the volatility channel

Discover why a fall or rise in IV may change the hedge target, even with spot held fixed. Study the difference between a single-strike shock and changing skew, as well as why an assumed long-versus-short dealer portfolio reverses the result.

Charm: the time channel

Follow delta drift as expiry approaches. Investigate 0DTE options, weekend passage, intraday clock effects and different settlement rules. The passage of time is measurable; a claim that it forced a particular market transaction is a separate proposition.

Gamma: the spot channel

Connect the better-known Gamma mechanism with Vanna and Charm in the same delta-change estimate, then examine the limits of treating a single exposure level as support, resistance or a price magnet.

The dealer-positioning problem

Public open interest shows outstanding contracts but not the identity or signed net risk of their holders. Learn to test several defensible inventory scenarios rather than mistaking an assumed sign for a known fact.

Six practical skills for serious market analysis

Read the volatility surface

Work with term structure, skew, fixed-strike changes and event-related IV repricing instead of treating a single volatility-index print as the entire market.

Build VEX/CEX maps

Understand sensitivity aggregation by strike, maturity, multiplier and inventory scenario, with clear labels for observed and inferred inputs.

Handle expiration correctly

Distinguish SPX, SPY and VIX-related expirations, near-zero maturity, overnight drift, OPEX roll-off and potentially replaced inventory.

Translate exposure into futures context

Explore SPX/NDX-to-futures hypotheses while respecting contract specifications, basis differences and actual hedge instrument uncertainty.

Define a valid no-trade

Use conditional setups and invalidation criteria. A strong process can reject a trade when assumptions, data, price behavior or execution costs disagree.

Test the signal, not the narrative

Use Python calculations, unit tests, recorded timestamps, historical replay and benchmarks to judge whether exposure context adds measurable value.

Four thought-provoking studies inside the book

Case A · Hypothetical one-contract hedge ledger

When price stands still and delta changes

A dealer is hypothetically short one call with a 100-share multiplier. Delta moves from 0.40 to 0.45 because IV changes, while the underlying remains at $100. The model's neutral hedge target changes from +40 to +45 shares.

Initial: −40 option delta + 40 stock = 0
Later: −45 option delta + 40 stock = −5
New target: +45 stock shares
Observed five-share purchase: Not established

The same example with the opposite inventory sign implies the opposite adjustment. Actual desk decisions may differ from the theoretical target.

Case B · Calendar-time attribution

Why a Monday gap is not proof of weekend Charm

Advance a synthetic option from Friday to Monday under unchanged spot and IV, then add the actual scenario's hypothetical price gap and volatility change separately. The exercise shows how to isolate time-only delta drift rather than claim the entire price move was caused by the clock.

Case C · Earnings volatility crush

When the stock rises but the call disappoints

A correct bullish view on the underlying can coexist with a disappointing call-option outcome after implied volatility collapses. Reprice the option and distinguish Vega-related premium losses, Delta exposure and Vanna-related delta changes.

Case D · Failed exposure anchor

What if price ignores the alleged magnet?

Mark a large Gamma or Charm concentration before the session, then record a case where price passes through the proposed anchor. Rather than moving the level afterward, preserve the failed hypothesis and investigate alternatives.

These are synthetic teaching cases, not verified historical demonstrations or profit statistics.

Frequently asked questions

What is Vanna?

Vanna measures an option's delta sensitivity to a change in implied volatility. The resulting portfolio exposure depends on quantities, position signs, multipliers and modeling conventions.

What is Charm?

Charm measures the change in delta with time. The book explicitly distinguishes calendar time moving forward from the positive time remaining to expiry.

How is Vanna different from Gamma?

Gamma measures delta sensitivity to the underlying price, while Vanna measures delta sensitivity to implied volatility. Charm measures delta sensitivity to time.

Does open interest tell us what dealers own?

No. Open interest alone does not disclose whether a dealer is net long or short. The book presents inventory assumptions as scenarios, not observed positions.

Does it cover 0DTE, OPEX and index futures?

Yes. The syllabus includes 0DTE, near-expiry Charm, SPX/NDX-to-futures exposure mapping, OPEX and VIXpiration.

Is Python included?

Yes. The book covers the data pipeline, Greek formulas, numerical tests, exposure dashboards and historical replay.

Does the book promise profits?

No. Teaching examples are synthetic unless independently identified as historical, and a computed exposure is not proof of a real hedge or a profitable trading strategy.
