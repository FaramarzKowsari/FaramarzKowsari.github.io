About this book

Inverse Fair Value Gaps (iFVG) starts with ordinary Fair Value Gap geometry and asks what changes when the original imbalance loses its role and price establishes itself on the opposite side. The useful information is the failed expectation, not merely a rectangle changing color. The book defines bullish and bearish iFVGs, then connects inversion to market structure, liquidity sweeps, displacement, premium/discount and dealing-range location.

Multi-timeframe analysis leads into three practical entry models, stops, invalidation, targets, risk and confluence without indicator overload. Four market environments, false iFVGs, failure modes, backtesting, a complete process and eight case studies keep the framework testable. FVG, iFVG, MSS and BOS are treated as practitioner terms whose exact conventions must be stated rather than assumed universal.

When an imbalance fails, the real trading question begins.

Inverse Fair Value Gaps (iFVG): Inverse Fair Value Gap Trading by Faramarz Kowsari takes a popular price-action pattern beyond the rectangle. An iFVG is interesting because an earlier directional expectation has been contradicted: price moves through a previously defined gap, establishes itself on the other side, and may later retest that same area from a new direction.

The book reconstructs the entire decision sequence: original Fair Value Gap → defined violation → acceptance → opposite-side retest → entry trigger → technical invalidation → risk-controlled review. It also explains when those events do not add up to a worthwhile trade. Instead of promising automatic support, resistance or a guaranteed reversal, it makes the interpretation specific enough to test.

Understand the failed gap, not just the pattern. The complete edition develops each stage with chart-based instruction, false setups, eight contrasting cases and a ten-page visual reference appendix.

What is an inverse Fair Value Gap?

Before a gap can become inverse, its original three-candle geometry must be correct. In a bullish FVG, Candle 3's low is above Candle 1's high. In a bearish FVG, Candle 3's high is below Candle 1's low. The resulting non-overlap establishes the boundaries. The size of the middle candle alone does not define the zone, and a visible gap does not prove hidden institutional order flow.

Bullish iFVG: bearish gap reclaimed

A bearish imbalance formed during a downward move. Later, price breaks and accepts above it, then approaches the old interval from above. That prior bearish FVG becomes a support-like reference only if the chosen violation, structure and risk conditions qualify.

Bearish iFVG: bullish gap lost

A bullish imbalance formed during an upward move. Later, price closes and accepts below it, then returns to the former zone from underneath. It becomes a possible resistance-like reference, not an automatic short signal.

The five-stage iFVG decision chain

  1. Preserve the original FVG. Mark the exact outer-candle price boundaries before the outcome unfolds.
  2. Specify what counts as a violation. A wick penetration, a decisive body close and a midpoint breach are different events, not interchangeable labels.
  3. Look for acceptance beyond the gap. The book evaluates follow-through, meaningful structure breaks and an unsuccessful return to the old side, under predefined rules.
  4. Classify the retest. Is it the first approach from the new side, or has price rotated through the same area repeatedly?
  5. Separate the setup from the trade. An entry needs an observable trigger, structural invalidation, feasible target and realistic costs.

A failed FVG does not mechanically become a successful inverse zone. The original idea has failed; the opposite-direction trade is a new hypothesis that still requires evidence.

Structure, liquidity and location: why context changes the decision

MSS, BOS and swing hierarchy

An iFVG connected to a meaningful Market Structure Shift (MSS) or Break of Structure (BOS) carries different information from one that appears in ordinary overlapping congestion. Chapters 5–6 require the trader to name the structural change, not simply add an acronym to a chart.

Liquidity sweeps and displacement

A move beyond a visible prior high, low or range boundary is observable; the motive behind it is not. The book studies the reaction after that event: displacement, a changed swing relationship, FVG violation and a possible retest.

Premium, discount and dealing ranges

The same geometric zone can be attractive near a relevant range extreme and weak in the middle of the range. Chapter 7 tests whether the next structural objective leaves enough reward after the stop and costs.

Higher- and lower-timeframe alignment

A lower-timeframe bullish inversion can sit directly beneath higher-timeframe resistance. Chapter 8 separates the broader directional and location map from the shorter-term execution trigger, reducing hindsight-driven certainty.

Three practical iFVG entry models: which trade-off do you choose?

Chapter 9 compares three distinct entries rather than treating an ideal screenshot as proof that one is best.

Model A — First retest

Enter at the first qualifying return to a confirmed inverse FVG. This can improve price and reduce distance to invalidation, but it deliberately sacrifices additional evidence at the retest.

Model B — Confirmation entry

Wait for a predefined reaction: for example, a lower-timeframe swing break or rejection followed by displacement. More evidence can mean later fills, worse price or a missed move.

Model C — Reclaim and retest

Let price re-establish control beyond a specified boundary and then trade a subsequent return. The method adds a structural test but may reduce frequency and increase stop distance.

How the book suggests comparing them

Apply all three models to the same fixed sample of qualifying iFVG events. Track actual entries, missed trades, stop distance, MAE, MFE, transaction costs and outcome in R. A prettier entry is not a superior expectancy estimate.

When a beautiful iFVG should still be rejected

Chapters 10–13 show why the quality of a zone and the quality of a proposed trade are not the same thing. The book gives repeated attention to explicit no-trade conditions:

  • Weak displacement: overlapping price action without a meaningful structural change.
  • Repeated retests: an over-traded interval treated as though it were a fresh role reversal.
  • Poor location: mid-range entry with little space to a defensible opposing objective.
  • Late confirmation: the move looks clear, but the invalidation is now far away.
  • Volatile execution: spread, slippage or a scheduled event invalidate the apparent reward.
  • Moving definitions: the FVG boundaries or required confirmation change after observing the result.

Technical invalidation comes before position size. If the honest stop is too far away, the book argues for reducing exposure or skipping the setup. It does not manufacture a convenient stop merely to produce an attractive reward-to-risk number.

Eight case studies, including setups that fail

Chapter 16 contrasts continuation and reversal settings, both bullish and bearish, along with liquidity-driven and multi-timeframe examples. One bearish-continuation case begins with a bullish FVG created during a corrective rally. Price then breaks beneath it, changes local structure and retests the former gap from below. The pattern can be technically valid yet still untradable if the nearest downside objective leaves too little reward.

A bullish-reversal example begins with a sweep below a prior session low, followed by bullish displacement, a local structural break and reclaim of a bearish FVG. The sweep itself is not the signal. The later price behavior supplies the evidence. Together the examples teach the reader to distinguish what was known at the decision point from what becomes obvious only after the trade ends.

Backtesting iFVGs without fooling yourself

Chapter 14 treats screenshots as teaching material, not a statistical sample. It asks the trader to freeze the definition of FVG, violation, acceptance, retest, entry, stop and target before collecting examples. Every qualifying occurrence, including failures, should enter the dataset. The journal records entry, stop, target, outcome in R-multiples, maximum adverse excursion (MAE), maximum favorable excursion (MFE), session, market environment, costs and rejected trades.

Rule changes are logged as new strategy versions. The revised method is tested on a different period instead of claiming that the development sample independently validates the revision. This discipline helps distinguish reproducible conditions from the seductive logic of a completed chart.

Frequently asked questions about iFVG trading

What is an inverse Fair Value Gap (iFVG)?

Within this ICT/SMC framework, an iFVG is an earlier Fair Value Gap whose original directional role has been violated. A later opposite-side retest is then evaluated as a fresh, conditional trading hypothesis.

Does a wick through an FVG confirm inversion?

No. The book distinguishes a wick penetration, a body close through the zone, and subsequent acceptance. A trader must specify the qualifying rule before evaluating outcomes.

How do bullish and bearish iFVGs differ?

A bullish iFVG reclaims a prior bearish FVG from below and later tests it from above. A bearish iFVG breaks below a prior bullish FVG and later tests it from beneath.

What are the three iFVG entry models?

First retest, confirmation entry, and reclaim-and-retest. Their trade-offs concern entry price, evidence, opportunity frequency and stop distance.

Does the book discuss false setups and backtesting?

Yes. It covers failure modes, first versus repeated tests, realistic execution costs, MAE/MFE, versioned rules and evaluation on new data.

Are the case studies verified trading results?

No verified track record is implied. The book identifies its illustrative charts as synthetic educational examples unless specifically labeled otherwise.

Who should read Inverse Fair Value Gaps?

Traders studying price action, Fair Value Gaps, ICT, Smart Money Concepts, liquidity sweeps and systematic trade review. The first four chapters establish the foundations.

Independent educational material, not financial advice. ICT/SMC terminology is a practitioner framework, not an exchange-defined instrument. Illustrative charts are synthetic unless stated otherwise. No setup guarantees a reaction, a profit or a particular win rate; leveraged trading carries substantial risk.
