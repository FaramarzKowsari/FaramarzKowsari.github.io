What happens when intelligence becomes available—and responsibility becomes harder to locate?

An AI system can imitate a familiar voice, rank a job applicant, infer a private vulnerability or act on a user's behalf. In each case, its ability may be easy to demonstrate. The harder question is whether that ability gives anyone the permission to use it—and who must answer when things go wrong.

Ethics in the Age of Artificial Intelligence by Faramarz Kowsari follows that fault line through 43 chapters in eight parts. It begins with the difference between ethics, law, safety and good manners; shows how familiar wrongs become faster and less visible with automated systems; then turns to emerging claims about human rights, cognitive autonomy and the moral boundaries of increasingly capable AI.

This is not a promise that a single moral framework can settle every conflict. The book considers how design defaults and commercial incentives affect attention, why explanation is not enough without a path to appeal, what happens when assistants become agents, and why the possible emergence of more general intelligence requires careful distinctions rather than confident predictions. Its underlying concern is practical: human judgment should remain visible at the points where technical choices change lives.

The examples range from synthetic emergency phone calls, automated hiring and workplace monitoring to AI tutors, emotional companions, dating systems, digital memorials, permissioned agents and the ethics of shutting down increasingly autonomous software.

The central distinction: what technology can do, what an organization may do, and what it ought to do are not the same question.

Eight questions at the heart of the book

1. When is a useful system still unethical?

A model may be accurate, legal and profitable yet distribute opportunity unfairly, expose private inference or make refusal practically impossible. Ethical review asks about affected people, power and remedies—not model accuracy alone.

2. Who is responsible when “the algorithm decided”?

Models are built, configured, purchased and deployed by people and institutions. Accountability demands traceable decisions and reviewable responsibilities instead of a fictional machine scapegoat.

3. Does a synthetic person change the meaning of consent?

A copied voice or face can persuade, deceive or outlive the subject. Identity, disclosure, authenticity and permission become central when realistic representations are easy to manufacture and redistribute.

4. What should a person be able to challenge?

A consequential automated decision should offer meaningful reasons, error correction and proportionate review. Fluency is not certainty, and explanation without an avenue for remedy may be ethically inadequate.

5. Can personalization cross into cognitive control?

Systems that predict preferences, emotions or vulnerabilities may influence people without ever asking them to disclose those traits. The book examines cognitive privacy, autonomy and the proposed freedom not to be optimized.

6. When does helpful automation make us less capable?

AI can support learning, work and relationships while also creating dependency or deskilling. The question is whether a tool strengthens the person's own competence and judgment or slowly displaces them.

7. What changes when an assistant can act?

Drafting a recommendation and sending money are ethically different acts. Agent permissions, logging, confirmation, reversible authority and credible stop mechanisms must scale with consequences.

8. How should we discuss AGI without pretending certainty?

General intelligence, subjective experience and moral status are different questions. The book treats future consciousness and possible machine rights as debates—not established attributes of present-day AI.

What readers can take into practice

The book's value lies in turning broad ethical principles into specific questions a reader can ask at work, at home and when designing or choosing technology.

  • Distinguish capability from legitimacy: an available technical feature does not automatically justify collecting data, imitating a person or delegating a decision.
  • Recognize engineered unfairness: investigate bias in training data, target definitions, defaults, incentives and the institutions using a model.
  • Question synthetic evidence: consider consent, provenance and independent verification when images, voice and video can be plausibly fabricated.
  • Demand usable explanations: ask for reasons that an affected person can understand, challenge and correct when the stakes justify them.
  • Protect cognitive and personal boundaries: identify sensitive inferences, manipulative personalization and uses of identity that exceed consent.
  • Use AI without surrendering competence: apply assistance in education, writing, coding and work while preserving human understanding and responsibility.
  • Set limits on autonomous agents: grant minimal permissions, make consequential actions reviewable, define confirmation points and establish stop authority.
  • Reason about uncertain futures: separate present risks from speculative claims about AGI, sentience and future moral status.

Five human situations that make AI ethics impossible to ignore

These illustrative situations reflect the subjects examined in the book. They show why ethics cannot be reduced to a software compliance checkbox.

Chapters 5, 14 and 26

When a synthetic voice sounds exactly like someone you love

A family receives an urgent call in a familiar voice asking for money. The immediate harm may be fraud; the lasting harm is that audio alone no longer carries the same evidentiary weight.

Questions worth asking: Who consented to the imitation? What second channel can verify a request? Who is responsible for making impersonation easier to distribute?

Chapters 7, 10, 12 and 13

When a rejected applicant can see a score but cannot appeal

A hiring or lending system provides a confident number while hiding the facts that influenced the decision. Apparent precision replaces the affected person's ability to correct a mistake.

Questions worth asking: Can the person obtain actionable reasons? Who can review incorrect inputs? Is the appeal route usable or merely decorative?

Chapters 18 and 19

When the best-performing tutor weakens the student's competence

A student completes every assignment with AI assistance and receives strong grades, yet may struggle to explain or perform the underlying task independently.

Questions worth asking: What is being learned, and what is being outsourced? Which abilities must a learner still demonstrate? Are educational outcomes measuring understanding or polished outputs?

Chapters 20 and 21

When a companion remembers more about a child than the family does

A highly personalized conversational system becomes central to a child's routines, preferences and emotional disclosures. A service optimized for engagement may acquire disproportionate influence.

Questions worth asking: What are the boundaries of retention, nudging and commercial use? Can the child and caregiver inspect or correct the model's inferences? Does the relationship support independent growth?

Chapters 28–32 and 42

When an agent acts before anyone has approved its next step

An agent can draft a proposal, message a customer, change permissions or initiate a financial action. More autonomy makes the difference between a suggestion and an external effect ethically decisive.

Questions worth asking: Who granted each permission? Which actions require confirmation? Can an authorized person observe, reverse, pause or stop the agent when necessary?

From abstract concern to a reviewable ethical decision

A recurring argument in the book is that a technically successful product can still fail the people subject to it. The table below translates some of its themes into practical design and governance questions.

A practical AI ethics checklist for readers, teams and decision-makers

Before deploying, purchasing, or relying on a consequential AI feature, translate ethical concern into a short review that can be revisited as circumstances change.

  1. Name the real decision

    What is the system allowed to classify, recommend, generate or execute?

  2. Identify those affected

    Who receives the benefit, who bears the risk, and who has least power to refuse?

  3. Ask about permission

    Is consent meaningful, proportionate and specific to the intended use?

  4. Inspect the objective

    Could optimizing the chosen metric reward manipulation, exclusion, dependency or deskilling?

  5. Preserve uncertainty

    Can the system acknowledge limits, abstain or request meaningful human review?

  6. Make decisions contestable

    Can affected people understand the basis of an important decision and seek correction or remedy?

  7. Constrain external action

    Are permissions narrow, actions logged, and consequential steps subject to confirmation?

  8. Design an ethical exit

    Who can pause, roll back, retire or stop the system, and what happens to people who depend on it?

One useful distinction: these are practical questions derived from the book's arguments, not a certification checklist, legal compliance test or universal formula for ethical decisions.

AGI, consciousness and machine rights: questions the book keeps open

Its later chapters deliberately separate questions that are often collapsed into one. General intelligence concerns capability across domains. Consciousness concerns the possibility of subjective experience. Moral status concerns which entities, if any, deserve consideration for their own sake. One claim does not establish the others.

The manuscript discusses disagreement about defining AGI, whose values a powerful system should reflect, what happens when legitimate human values conflict, and whether hypothetical future sentient AI might raise new obligations. It does not treat impressive model performance as proof of feeling or personhood. Its near-term emphasis remains on responsibilities people and institutions can identify today.

That distinction helps avoid both complacency and science-fiction certainty. We can design meaningful oversight, reversibility and accountability now without pretending that debates about future intelligence have already been settled.

Frequently asked questions about the book

What makes this AI ethics book different from a general introduction to artificial intelligence?

Rather than concentrating on model mechanics, it asks how prediction, imitation and delegated action change consent, responsibility, privacy, justice and human agency. Its 43 chapters use concrete dilemmas from work, family life, institutions and emerging autonomous systems.

Does the book argue that AI itself is morally responsible for harmful decisions?

It treats AI behavior as ethically consequential without assuming today's systems bear human-style moral responsibility. Designers, deployers, decision-makers, institutions and their incentives remain central to accountability.

Why are deepfakes a larger problem than simple fake pictures?

Synthetic audio, images and video can impersonate people, facilitate fraud and weaken shared trust in evidence. The book discusses consent, provenance and practical verification as responses, while avoiding claims that any single technical safeguard is foolproof.

What is the relationship between explainability and contestability?

An explanation identifies relevant reasons behind a consequential decision; contestability creates a usable path to challenge an error, obtain review or seek remedy. A plausible explanation without a meaningful appeal process is often inadequate.

Does the book discuss rights such as cognitive privacy and the right not to be simulated?

Yes. The book examines these as emerging ethical claims and proposed protections; it does not present all of them as universally established legal rights.

How does AI affect students, jobs and relationships?

The book explores when assistance becomes replacement: automated homework that weakens learning, working lives reshaped by deskilling, emotional dependence on AI companions, algorithmic dating, parenting and synthetic memorialization.

What changes when AI systems become autonomous agents?

The ethical burden expands from evaluating answers to constraining real actions. The book discusses permissions, bounded delegation, logs, intervention, refusal, mistaken objectives and the ability to stop a system safely.

Does the book claim that artificial general intelligence or conscious AI already exists?

No. It treats AGI definitions as contested and explores machine consciousness and future rights as open questions rather than established facts about current AI systems.

Who is this book written for?

Students, educators, parents, software developers, product teams, researchers, business leaders, policymakers and general readers who want to make more informed decisions about the uses and limits of AI.

Who should read this book?

For readers who live with AI: students, teachers, parents and everyday users who want to recognize when convenience changes privacy, trust, learning or personal autonomy.

For readers who build and deploy AI: developers, designers, product managers, entrepreneurs and organizational leaders who must translate consent, accountability and appropriate oversight into meaningful decisions.

For readers who evaluate AI's social future: researchers, policymakers and general readers interested in fairness, governance, human rights, emerging agents and the unsettled questions around AGI.

The writing is introductory but intellectually serious. Its primary contribution is a way of framing difficult ethical questions clearly, not a claim to replace domain-specific law, safety assessment or professional advice.
