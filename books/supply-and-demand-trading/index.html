About this book

A familiar chart pattern creates a tempting story: price leaves a narrow base, returns days later, and appears ready to bounce from the same rectangle. But what, precisely, does that rectangle prove? SUPPLY AND DEMAND TRADING begins at the point where many zone-trading explanations stop. It treats a price zone as a testable trading hypothesis, not as evidence that unseen institutions have committed to defend a level. The book's opening chapters distinguish the economic idea of supply and demand from supply-and-demand zones on a candlestick chart, then introduce the bid-ask spread, the order book, price discovery, auction mechanics, volatility and the conditions under which historical prices can become useful references. The result is a sharper question than 'Does this look like demand?': 'What can the data establish, and what must the next trade prove?'

The 42-chapter progression builds from market mechanics to zone construction. Readers learn to define bases and departures, compare common departure structures, set boundaries before the future becomes visible, and document freshness, retests, aging, nested zones, opposing zones and objective invalidation. The next layer adds swing structure, market regimes, the multi-timeframe map, available space to targets, trend versus range context, and scheduled economic events. Rather than placing a trade because a rectangle has been drawn, the reader develops a reason to prefer one setup over another, or to reject an attractive-looking setup entirely. This is where the book distinguishes zone location from trade execution: a meaningful price reference is not automatically a filled order, and a strong reaction on a chart is not automatically a profitable trade.

Six distinct trading propositions receive their own decision rules: a first-return limit order, lower-timeframe confirmation, continuation after an impulsive departure, a range-boundary reversal, a breakout and retest, and a setup constructed around a failed zone. Each carries different timing, cancellation, invalidation and fill assumptions. Execution chapters put the stop, target, position size, contract or share quantity, trading friction and portfolio exposure into the same calculation. Market and limit orders are compared as operational choices rather than visual preferences. The discussion also asks what happens when a trade is missed, spreads widen, a stop slips, or several nominally separate positions concentrate the same risk.

The scope extends beyond one chart or market. Later chapters connect zone analysis with candlesticks, volume profile, volume-weighted prices, Depth of Market, footprint displays, delta and Smart Money Concepts terminology, always asking whether additional information truly changes a decision. Separate adaptations address equities and ETFs, centralized index futures, broker-dependent spot forex and CFDs, gold and silver, and crypto spot and perpetual contracts. These differences matter: a zone inferred from candles is not the same kind of information as a displayed futures order book, and one cannot simply transfer execution assumptions from one venue to another.

The book ends where a serious trading method begins: with a rulebook another trader could follow, a record of decisions made before outcomes were known, replay and data-quality controls, backtest design, statistical validation, forward testing, failure review and an annotated casebook. Practical appendices include a market-and-zone glossary, zone-marking worksheet, trade-planning and position-size sheets, research-data schema and reference register. Readers looking for an infallible zone will not find one. Readers who want to turn chart intuition into definitions, executable orders, bounded risk and falsifiable evidence will find a substantial working framework.

How many chapters are described in SUPPLY AND DEMAND TRADING?

The source contents list 42 consecutive numbered chapters, beginning with “WHO MOVES PRICE IN A TWO SIDED MARKET” and ending with “FORWARD TESTING FAILURE REVIEW AND THE.” The complete edition expands the topics through the author's full explanations and figures.

Does this book include THE TWO MEANINGS OF SUPPLY AND DEMAND?

Yes. The original contents identify it as Chapter 2.

Where is WHEN A ZONE FAILS covered?

The manuscript lists this as Chapter 22, following earlier concepts and before the final applications.

What will I study in the later chapters?

The final numbered topics include REPLAY DATA QUALITY AND A DECISION JOURNAL; BACKTEST DESIGN AND STATISTICAL VALIDATION; FORWARD TESTING FAILURE REVIEW AND THE.

Does learning these concepts guarantee successful trades?

No. A chart setup may fail and live performance is affected by risk, execution, leverage and market changes. This is educational information, not individualized advice.

What you will learn

  • Explain what candles and exchange data actually reveal, and why a zone cannot prove invisible institutional orders are waiting.
  • Define and mark supply and demand zones with reproducible boundaries before seeing the outcome.
  • Assess departure structure, zone freshness, retests, nested areas, opposing zones and invalidation without hindsight.
  • Read zones inside swing structure, multi-timeframe context, market regime, available target space and scheduled-event risk.
  • Differentiate six setup families and write their entry, cancellation, stop and failure rules before submitting an order.
  • Recognize when a zone is a reference rather than a complete trade, and why a missed limit fill must not be reported as a winner.
  • Calculate trade risk in account currency using instrument units, quantity, stop distance, spreads, slippage and fees.
  • Compare market and limit execution, understand partial fills and keep portfolio-level concentration visible.
  • Use candlestick behavior, volume profile, VWAP, footprint, DOM and delta only when they provide measurable additional evidence.
  • Adapt analysis to stocks, exchange-traded futures, OTC forex, commodities and crypto without assuming identical data or fill mechanics.
  • Document a rulebook, decision journal and annotated cases so another trader can reproduce the method.
  • Design backtests with all eligible candidates, realistic execution assumptions, no future-data leakage and honest negative results.
  • Use forward testing, failure review and no-trade rules to improve process rather than chasing attractive historical examples.

Who this book is for

For discretionary price-action traders, Smart Money Concepts readers, forex and CFD traders, futures and equity traders, crypto-market participants, and systematic researchers who want a rigorous bridge from drawing price zones to making reproducible trading decisions. The book is especially useful for readers familiar with support/resistance who now want to understand zone formation, distinguish multiple entry models, calculate realistic execution risk and test whether their rules survive evidence rather than relying on screenshots of winning trades. It is educational material, not a promise of trading returns.
