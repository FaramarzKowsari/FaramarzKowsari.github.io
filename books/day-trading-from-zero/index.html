About this book

Day Trading from Zero is a beginner roadmap built around essential market principles, risk discipline, chart reading, strategy testing and prompt engineering. It explicitly treats AI as a learning assistant, chart-review partner, journaling coach and structured-thinking tool—not as an oracle or permission to risk money. The book encourages simulation, journaling and evidence before live trading and repeatedly warns that leverage and day trading can produce rapid losses.

A central goal is to build a written trading plan that can actually be tested. The plan defines what to trade, when to trade, why to enter, where the thesis fails, how much to risk, how to exit and which conditions require standing aside. The book develops a minimum viable strategy with observable rules for market condition, location, trigger, stop, target, time of day and management, helping beginners replace strategy shopping and emotional improvisation with one repeatable learning process.

What is inside Day Trading from Zero?

The source manuscript develops 12 numbered topics, moving from What Day Trading Really Is to The Essential Technical Analysis Toolkit and ultimately A Ninety-Day Roadmap from Beginner to Prepared. The reading sequence gives a prospective buyer a more useful view than a list of indicators: it shows where the framework starts and how the later analytical and practical questions build on it.

A beginner's 90-day learning experiment

The book's roadmap is deliberately less glamorous than a list of winning indicators. It asks the beginner to earn each next step with evidence. Here is a practical reading of Chapter 12, in which the objective is preparedness rather than earning income in ninety days.

Days 1–15 · Foundations

Learn candles, trend and range, fees, order types, stops, basic sizing and journaling. Describe one chart each day without predicting. The book recommends using AI as a tutor that explains and quizzes, not an entry engine.

Days 16–35 · One setup and backtesting

Pick one of the book's candidate models (for example, breakout and retest), define market condition, trigger, invalidation, target and no-trade rule. Manually examine at least 50 historical cases, including failures and tempting but ineligible examples.

Days 36–65 · Simulate the decisions

Trade the same written rules in simulation, keep the screenshots and a journal, and track every rule violation. If rule breaches remain frequent, return to practice rather than advancing because the equity curve temporarily looks attractive.

Days 66–90 · Review and choose

Audit results and conduct before outcome. If the setup remains unclear, revise and test; if it is stable, the book describes an optional tiny live probation period under strict limits, not an automatic graduation to funded trading.

Example audit from the book's approach: A beginner chooses a breakout-and-retest model but cannot say whether a wick counts as a valid breakout. This is not yet a testable setup. The next action is to define close-versus-wick, retest timing, structural stop, excluded news sessions and the maximum acceptable risk before counting trades. A beautiful after-the-fact chart cannot repair unclear rules.

Practical questions readers ask

Do I need to buy a course or start with live capital?

The source explicitly starts with a notebook, chart observation and simulation. It does not require live trading during the foundation and testing stages.

Why does the roadmap ask for fifty historical cases?

To expose a setup to winners, losers, and no-trade opportunities before deciding whether simulation deserves attention. Fifty observations do not establish statistical certainty.

Is ninety days enough to become profitable?

The chapter states the goal is organization and preparedness, not mastery or a guaranteed income stream.

What you will learn

  • Build day-trading knowledge from risk, chart reading and repeatable process rather than from promises of quick profit.
  • Use AI for learning, review and structured questioning without delegating trading responsibility to the model.
  • Create a written trading plan with market, session, trigger, stop, target, management and no-trade rules.
  • Turn vague strategy language into observable conditions that can be backtested and reviewed.
  • Practice in simulation and journal evidence before increasing exposure or complexity.
