We've Lived With Dogs for at Least 16,000 Years. Why Are We Still Misreading Them?

A remarkable human–animal partnership. An everyday language we still too often misunderstand.

A tail wags. A dog turns away. Someone reaches closer and mistakes silence for consent. What if the most important part of the conversation is what happens between those familiar gestures? This thought-provoking essay explores why knowing dog body language is essential for compassionate, safer coexistence—from family pets and working dogs to free-roaming dogs in our cities.

Inspired by THE COMPLETE VISUAL ATLAS OF DOG BODY LANGUAGE by Faramarz Kowsari, the article takes readers beyond the popular one-gesture-one-meaning myths. It explains how eyes, ears, mouth, tail carriage, posture, movement, vocalisations and social distance interact, and why interpretation must also account for the individual dog, the sequence of events, health, pain, hunger, heat, fatigue and environmental context.

Canine Communication & Animal Behaviour

A dog is speaking long before you decide what the movement means.

Reading a dog is not a matter of translating one ear position, one wag or one bark into a fixed emotional label. The same outward motion can accompany different experiences, and a single photograph cannot reveal what happened immediately before or after it. This atlas starts where careful observation starts: with the individual dog, the anatomy of the movement and the circumstances around it.

THE COMPLETE VISUAL ATLAS OF DOG BODY LANGUAGE by Faramarz Kowsari follows canine communication from precise facial and bodily observations to interactions unfolding through time. Eyes, gaze, ears, muzzle, teeth displays, lips, tail carriage, muscle tension and gait are considered alongside sounds, scent, touch and personal space. The emphasis is on signals in combination rather than a dictionary of isolated gestures.

Later chapters examine greetings, play, avoidance, conflict, resource-related tension, human–dog interactions, children, clinics, ageing, pain and situations where the most useful response is to give a dog space. The reader learns to separate what can be directly seen from an interpretation that still needs context, comparison and humility.

A closer look

The anatomy of a better interpretation

The book’s observational method insists on three distinct questions before assigning an emotional story.

NOTICE

What moved?

Describe ear direction, eyes, lip corners, weight shift, tail base and sound without assigning an intention.

CONNECT

What was happening?

Consider the complete posture, the other participants, distance, health, available exit routes and the preceding sequence.

RESPOND

What is safe and respectful?

Adapt distance, stop intrusive handling or seek qualified veterinary/behavioural assessment when warning signs warrant it.

One gesture, different stories

A dog may lick its lips near food and repeat the same movement during uncomfortable handling. Seeing the action is straightforward; inferring its function requires the rest of the body, the situation and the sequence. The atlas builds this distinction into its method, rather than treating one signal as a guaranteed translation.

Who This Book Is For

Dog owners, pet professionals, canine-behaviour students, trainers, shelter staff, veterinary team members and anyone who wants to observe canine interactions more carefully. It is an educational behavioural reference, not a diagnostic or bite-safety guarantee.

Scope and care

The book does not diagnose a dog's mental or physical health. Sudden behaviour changes, pain, breathing problems or immediate bite danger require appropriate professional attention. Never provoke a warning to test an interpretation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a wagging tail always mean a friendly dog?

No. Tail carriage, motion, muscle tension, approach, distance, social setting and the individual dog's baseline must be interpreted together.

Does the book focus only on facial expressions?

No. Its 60 chapters cover eyes, ears, mouth, tail, posture, movement, vocalisations, scent, social distance, dog–dog and dog–human encounters, stress, age and health context.

Can a photograph tell me whether a dog will bite?

No. A still image lacks the full sequence and context. Safety decisions must be conservative when a dog is fearful, cornered or threatening.

Is this suitable for owners as well as professionals?

Yes. The book aims to help readers describe observable canine behaviour precisely and distinguish plausible explanations from certainty.
