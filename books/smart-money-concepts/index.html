Stop collecting chart labels. Start building a trading decision you can explain.

Every trader can draw a rectangle around a Fair Value Gap or circle a swing high after the move. The harder skill is deciding, with the future still hidden, which level matters, whether a liquidity event changes the structure, and where your idea becomes wrong. Smart Money Concepts by Faramarz Kowsari is built around that harder skill.

This book develops an organized, visual approach to Smart Money Concepts (SMC), price action, market structure, liquidity sweeps, BOS, CHoCH, MSS, displacement, Order Blocks, Fair Value Gaps, dealing ranges and trade execution. It connects those ideas to long and short setups, entries, stop-loss placement, risk, sessions, news volatility, backtesting and journaling. The objective is to make the trader's reasoning observable and reviewable rather than to claim knowledge of what an unseen institution intends.

Across 40 chapters, practical blank-chart exercises, four contrasting case studies and a ten-page visual SMC atlas, the manuscript asks one question repeatedly: what changes in your decision when this evidence appears? An answer that only adds another impressive label is not enough.

What sets this guide apart? It treats a failed setup, a withheld entry and an unclear chart as meaningful educational outcomes. The subtitle's “high-probability” language describes a search for better-qualified setups—not a measured or guaranteed win rate.

From price-action vocabulary to a repeatable analytical process

Readers progress from the basic grammar of swings to the more difficult question of whether a complete trading idea deserves risk. The following skills are developed across the source manuscript:

  • Read market structure: meaningful swing highs and lows, HH/HL and LH/LL sequences, BOS, candidate CHoCH, and better-supported MSS transitions.
  • Map liquidity without mythology: BSL, SSL, near-equal highs and lows, external/internal references, sweeps, grabs and inducement as hypotheses grounded in observable price.
  • Interpret displacement and imbalance: distinguish forceful repricing from an isolated large candle; place an FVG in a structural context instead of trading the gap by itself.
  • Evaluate Order Blocks: inspect origin, location, consequence, refinement, breaker and mitigation ideas while expecting zones to fail sometimes.
  • Trade from a declared range: identify equilibrium, premium/discount and higher-timeframe context before treating a retracement as a potential entry.
  • Write both long and short plans: order evidence by context, liquidity, movement, transition, retracement, invalidation and objective.
  • Design defensible risk: position sizing, stop-loss location, liquidity objectives, slippage, leverage, correlated exposure and event risk.
  • Test and review independently: a personal playbook, backtesting rules, trade journal, failed-case analysis, no-trade decisions and unlabeled-chart reading.

Eight essential Smart Money Concepts explained in context

STRUCTURE

BOS, CHoCH & MSS

A structure label only becomes informative after selecting the relevant swing reference and the state it may confirm or interrupt. Later price movement must not decide which earlier break “counts.”

LIQUIDITY

BSL, SSL & equal highs/lows

Visible highs and lows may attract orders, stops and attention. They are useful places to study reaction and acceptance, not guaranteed magnets or proof of an institution's hidden plan.

PRICE DELIVERY

Displacement

A forceful leg can change structure or bring price into a new area. Compare its scale and follow-through with nearby volatility before inferring structural significance.

IMBALANCE

Fair Value Gaps (FVGs)

The geometry of a three-candle gap is straightforward. Whether it is fresh, relevant and tradable depends on context, reaction, invalidation and available objectives.

POINT OF INTEREST

Order Blocks (OBs)

An OB is a contextual candidate, not every opposite-color candle. The book also investigates refinement, failed zones, breaker blocks and mitigation.

LOCATION

Premium, discount & ranges

The midpoint of an arbitrary range is arbitrary too. Declare a meaningful dealing range first, then ask what other evidence turns a location into a defensible plan.

MULTI-TIMEFRAME

Top-down analysis

Higher, intermediate and lower charts have different jobs. Local reversal evidence need not erase the broader structural state; timeframe conflicts should be made explicit.

EXECUTION

Invalidation & no-trade gates

Potential reward means little if the idea's failure condition is unknown, the spread consumes the edge, or the evidence cannot survive scrutiny before entry.

The 10-question SMC Decision Framework

The book ends its core teaching with an ordered decision sequence. This website presents the framework in condensed form so prospective readers can see how the concepts fit together. It is useful precisely because a failed earlier gate cannot be rescued by adding more labels later.

  1. Where is price?

    Declare the active dealing range and the timeframe that governs the decision.

  2. Where is meaningful liquidity?

    Mark relevant internal and external high/low references—not every visible candle.

  3. Has liquidity actually been taken?

    Distinguish an approach from a trade-through, a rejection or sustained acceptance.

  4. Did meaningful displacement occur?

    Compare the move with recent conditions and check if structure was materially affected.

  5. Did structure confirm continuation or transition?

    Classify BOS, CHoCH or MSS only after choosing the reference level in advance.

  6. Where is the retracement area?

    Select an FVG, OB or other zone because it belongs to the causal sequence.

  7. Where is invalidation?

    Describe the exact condition that makes the thesis wrong before entering.

  8. Where is the next defensible objective?

    Name a structure or liquidity destination without presuming it will be reached.

  9. Does the trade justify the risk?

    Include size, stop distance, spread, slippage, leverage, correlation and event risk.

  10. Is no trade the cleaner decision?

    Stay flat when a material gate fails or the evidence conflicts.

One principle ties the sequence together: observe the event first, interpret it second, decide whether to act third. A trade can be visually elegant and still not pass the risk test.

Four examples that show what disciplined chart reading looks like

The following illustrative scenarios translate the book's bullish, bearish, failed and no-trade case-study themes into the questions a trader would ask before knowing the result. They are not historical price records or executable trade recommendations.

Scenario A · Bullish reasoning

A long thesis built before the bounce

What is visible: A higher-timeframe bullish context is identifiable; a visible lower liquidity reference is traded through and price later shows directional repricing. A retracement area sits within the declared dealing range.

What to decide: Ask whether the structure reference, displacement and reaction form a coherent sequence. Define invalidation below the relevant reference and compare the possible objective with the actual risk.

When the thesis fails: If the proposed zone is reached without confirmation, price violates the protected low, or available reward is too small for the risk, the long thesis may not justify execution.

Scenario B · Bearish reasoning

A short idea is not created by an isolated upper wick

What is visible: Price probes a previously visible high, but a wick by itself cannot establish that the market has reversed. The broader state and subsequent reaction still matter.

What to decide: Look for evidence of rejection, meaningful downward displacement and structural weakness. Only then compare a possible retracement entry with a prewritten invalidation and objective.

When the thesis fails: A clean acceptance above the high, missing structural evidence or event-driven volatility can weaken or cancel the thesis.

Scenario C · Failed setup

A convincing FVG still fails

What is visible: An imbalance and a contextual zone look attractive, but the later reaction does not produce the anticipated movement. A trader is tempted to change the original zone or rename the event after the fact.

What to decide: Keep the timestamped entry conditions and invalidation unchanged in the journal, separate process adherence from outcome, and ask whether a frozen rule set survives a larger sample.

When the thesis fails: A loss does not prove every SMC concept false; a winning result does not validate an undisciplined decision. The useful question is whether the original reasoning was reproducible.

Scenario D · No-trade discipline

Sometimes the chart's best signal is uncertainty

What is visible: A lower-timeframe pattern appears while a higher timeframe disagrees, the proposed stop is unclear or a nearby session/news event creates execution risk.

What to decide: Apply the predeclared veto gates. If meaningful context, invalidation or reward-to-risk cannot be defended, remain flat and document why.

When the thesis fails: The cost of skipping one move is visible; the cost of habitually abandoning risk rules may be much larger. The no-trade classification is a deliberate decision, not a missed setup.

These examples help answer the book's recurring challenge: if a setup wins or loses, can the original reasoning still be reconstructed without changing the rules?

The practical exercises: from blank charts to a personal trading playbook

Read without labels

Begin on an unannotated chart. Mark only decisive highs, lows and zones, explain your reference selection, and name the next observation that would change the hypothesis.

Write the thesis before the trigger

Record context, intended direction, entry conditions, invalidation and target. Reveal subsequent price action only after the decision point is documented.

Freeze rules during testing

Keep setup definitions constant for a test batch. Consider sample size, average outcomes, adverse/favorable excursion, drawdown and transaction costs—not win rate alone.

Journal mistakes as well as results

Capture screenshots, rule violations, execution quality and trade management. A profitable rule violation is still a rule violation; a planned loss can still be analytically useful.

Smart Money Concepts book: frequently asked questions

What are Smart Money Concepts (SMC) in trading?

SMC is a practitioner framework for studying market structure, visible liquidity references, displacement, imbalances, order-block candidates and trade location. This book treats the concepts as testable chart interpretations, not a way to know institutional motives.

Does the book teach Break of Structure (BOS), CHoCH and MSS?

Yes. Chapters 5 and 6 address structural breaks, candidate changes of character and market structure shifts, with emphasis on selecting the meaningful reference before the outcome is visible.

How does the book explain buy-side and sell-side liquidity?

Chapters 7–10 and 21 explore visible highs and lows, near-equal extremes, internal and external liquidity, sweeps and inducement. A visible liquidity pool is a plausible reference, never a guaranteed destination.

Are Fair Value Gaps and Order Blocks reliable buy or sell signals by themselves?

No. The book teaches how to describe FVG geometry and evaluate order-block candidates in context; reaction, timeframe, invalidation, execution risk and competing evidence still determine whether a plan is defensible.

What is the difference between premium and discount in SMC?

They refer to the upper and lower halves of a specifically selected dealing range. Without a declared range and relevant structural context, a 50% line alone does not justify a trade.

Does the book cover practical entry, stop-loss and take-profit planning?

Yes. Chapters 22–28 address confluence, long and short trade narratives, entry models, invalidation, liquidity-based objectives, position sizing and risk management.

Does the book teach backtesting and building a trading journal?

Yes. Chapters 33–35 discuss building a personal playbook, freezing definitions before a test batch, tracking outcomes beyond win rate, and recording decisions and rule violations.

Does it include examples of losing trades or avoiding trades?

Yes. Chapters 36–39 are a bullish case, a bearish case, a failed-setup case and a no-trade case. Failure and abstention are part of the book's method rather than afterthoughts.

Are the charts historical, independently verified market data?

The published manuscript identifies its chart figures as synthetic educational schematics unless explicitly labeled otherwise. The schematics clarify structural relationships; they are not evidence that a strategy produced historical returns.

Does Smart Money Concepts guarantee high-probability or profitable trades?

No. The subtitle refers to conditional setup design, not an established probability of success or a performance guarantee. The book explicitly warns about execution costs, leverage and the possibility of loss.

How many chapters are included, and can I preview the ebook?

The Google Play edition has 220 pages and the manuscript contains 40 chapters, followed by a ten-step decision framework, glossary and ten-page visual SMC atlas. Google Play Books provides a free sample, subject to its preview settings.

Who will benefit most from reading it?

Newer price-action and SMC learners who need the relationships among swings, liquidity, BOS/CHoCH, FVGs and OBs explained in a consistent sequence instead of scattered online jargon.

Intermediate discretionary traders whose charts are crowded with confirmations but whose entry rules, invalidation and review process remain difficult to state precisely.

Systematic or algorithmically minded researchers who want to convert chart vocabulary into testable definitions, journal fields and hypotheses that can be evaluated without outcome-based relabeling.

The material can inform chart-reading research in forex, futures, stocks, indices, crypto and other liquid markets, but asset-specific execution costs, session definitions, leverage and risks still require independent attention.
