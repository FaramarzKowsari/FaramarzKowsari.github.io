About the book

THINK LIKE THE WEALTHY begins with a subtle observation: a financial decision often starts before the first calculation. It starts with the question we accept. “How can I afford this?” assumes the purchase deserves our attention. “What problem am I trying to solve?” creates room for alternatives. The difference is especially powerful when advertisements, colleagues or status comparisons have quietly supplied the goal.

Faramarz Kowsari builds the book around twenty questions that help readers examine their assumptions about success, the incentives behind recommendations, the real costs of purchases, the opportunity cost of time and the evidence required to commit to a plan. The approach does not imagine that all wealthy people share a secret psychology; instead, it investigates reasoning habits that may help anyone make more deliberate choices within their actual constraints.

The chapters move from reframing a problem to measuring what matters, identifying bottlenecks and challenging favorite beliefs. Later inquiries ask whether a decision is reversible, who bears its consequences, what assumptions it makes about tomorrow and whether a familiar question has ceased to be useful. Fictional people face purchases, delayed invoices, job decisions, family obligations and business setbacks, so abstract reasoning becomes visible in ordinary economic life.

A concluding twenty-question field guide and thirty-day inquiry practice help readers turn the questions into a personal decision toolkit. This is financial psychology without a promise of easy riches: thoughtful questions cannot replace resources, fair institutions or expert advice, but they can improve what we notice before an expensive choice becomes difficult to reverse.

What readers will learn

  • Spot a financial question framed by an advertisement, comparison or inherited assumption.
  • Replace “Why me?” with an actionable question without minimizing real hardship.
  • Separate missing money from missing time, information, access or capacity.
  • Assess incentives and total ownership costs before a purchase.
  • Identify bottlenecks, opportunity costs and weak evidence in a proposed plan.
  • Ask what evidence would change your mind and whether a decision is reversible.
  • Consider second-order effects and the interests of people affected by your choice.
  • Build a shorter personal question deck and revisit it with a thirty-day practice.

Inside the book: a car purchase and an invisible assumption

Mira, a fictional new professional, begins looking at premium car financing after colleagues associate a certain vehicle with career success. The question on her mind is how to fit the monthly payments into her salary. The book asks a prior question: what would make her commute reliable, enjoyable and financially sustainable? That reformulation introduces options that the showroom's financing conversation never considered. Other constructed scenarios include a shopkeeper facing a broken refrigerator and a worker confronting delayed invoices.

All named scenes and numerical examples described in the book are fictional teaching illustrations unless explicitly stated otherwise.

Part 1: Reframe the problem

  1. Who Wrote the Question?Recognize how advertising, status and inherited assumptions can define a purchase before you evaluate it.
  2. From Why Me? to What Now?Move from an unanswerable complaint toward a verifiable next action without denying real hardship.
  3. What Is Actually Missing?Distinguish a cash shortage from missing time, access, information or capacity.
  4. Whose Definition of Rich?Examine whose standards of success are shaping your goals.
  5. What If the Obvious Answer Is Wrong?Generate plausible alternatives before committing to the first familiar solution.

Part 2: Notice what the first question hides

  1. What Can Be Measured Here?Seek concrete signals instead of relying on an impressive narrative.
  2. Who Benefits from My Choice?Investigate the incentives behind a recommendation or sales pitch.
  3. What Happens After the Purchase?Count ongoing ownership costs rather than only the checkout price.
  4. What Else Could This Hour Buy?Make the opportunity cost of time visible.
  5. Where Is the Bottleneck?Find the limiting step in an income, work or business process.

Part 3: Test the evidence and the consequences

  1. What Would a Beginner Notice?Challenge the assumptions that accumulated experience can hide.
  2. What Evidence Would Change My Mind?State in advance which facts would justify changing course.
  3. Is This Reversible?Assess how easily an uncertain commitment could be undone.
  4. What Is the Second Consequence?Explore what happens after the immediate benefit or cost.
  5. What Is the Smallest Honest Test?Find a limited, ethical way to investigate a decision before scaling it.

Part 4: Make the questions a practice

  1. Who Is Missing from the Room?Include people who must live with the consequences of your choice.
  2. What Am I Assuming About Tomorrow?Inspect forecasts and plans for fragile expectations about the future.
  3. What Could I Stop Asking?Retire questions that reward anxiety, comparison or unproductive repetition.
  4. The Twenty-Question Field GuideOrganize the questions into a personal decision toolkit.
  5. Ask Differently for Thirty DaysUse a month-long practice to turn questioning into a repeatable habit.

Try one question before your next financial commitment

Use this as a preview of the book's practical spirit; it is an adaptation rather than a substitute for the full text.

  1. Write the question you are currently trying to answer.
  2. Ask who or what supplied that framing.
  3. Describe the underlying need without naming the purchase or solution.
  4. List at least two different options, including doing nothing for now.
  5. Write the evidence, costs and affected people you would need to consider.
  6. Choose the next responsible step; revisit the decision after new information arrives.

Frequently asked questions

Does this book claim wealthy people all think the same way?

No. The introduction explicitly rejects that stereotype. The emphasis is on useful questions, evidence and choices rather than an alleged universal psychology of rich people.

Are the twenty questions relevant if I am not wealthy?

Yes. The prompts address everyday purchasing, work, opportunity, constraints, information and commitments. They do not assume the reader already has substantial assets.

Is this a conventional budgeting or investment handbook?

No. It is a financial decision-making and mindset book. It may help frame budgeting, career or investing questions, but does not prescribe individual financial products.

Does the book use examples?

Yes. Fictional teaching scenes cover a car purchase, a business equipment problem, overdue invoices and other real-life kinds of choices. These are illustrations, not testimonials.

Is there a practical system at the end?

Yes. The book includes a twenty-question field guide and a thirty-day practice designed to help readers apply its questions in daily decisions.

What is the main difference from THE ACTION-BASED MILLIONAIRE MINDSET?

THINK LIKE THE WEALTHY concentrates on asking and reframing the right questions. THE ACTION-BASED MILLIONAIRE MINDSET takes the next step: conducting bounded experiments, measuring results and deciding what to keep or stop.
